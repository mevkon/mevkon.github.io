Most smart-home apps shout — bright toggles, hard borders, colour everywhere. Demama asks a quieter question: can state and hierarchy be read through depth alone?

The answer: a soft-UI system on one rule — depth carries meaning. Pressed means chosen, raised means available, a single accent does the rest. Every screen speaks that one language.

01

The problem

Smart-home control surfaces have quietly become some of the noisiest screens we own.

Too much shouting for a room that should feel calm.

A modern home app juggles lights, climate, scenes, cameras and energy — and most solve it by adding more colour, more borders, more toggles. The result feels busier than the home it controls.

Status looks like control

A label that only reports state is styled like a switch — so people tap things that were never meant to be tapped.

Colour overload

Every function fights for attention with its own hue, so nothing actually stands out and the eye has nowhere to rest.

No shared language

Each screen invents its own controls, so what you learn on the thermostat doesn't transfer to lighting or scenes.

Busier than the home

The interface meant to bring calm adds cognitive load instead — you manage the app as much as the house.

02

Reading the market

Before drawing a single screen, I looked at how the three best-known smart-home apps handle the same job — and where they leave users stranded.

Google Home

Broad ecosystem
+Recent redesign trimmed navigation to three tabs and surfaces favourites fast.
–State and control blur together; users tap things meant only to report status.
–Focus drifted toward AI features while basic state reading stayed noisy.

Apple Home

Calm but shallow
+Clean, restrained look that doesn't shout — a calm starting point.
–Dense screens get cluttered fast and are hard to scan at a glance.
–No visual read on data from devices — numbers without shape or hierarchy.

SmartThings

Powerful, dense
+Enormous device coverage — one app for hundreds of brands.
–Visually crowded; finding a single device takes real effort.
–Each area speaks its own visual language, so nothing transfers between screens.
What I took from it

The same three faults repeat everywhere: controls and status look identical, screens grow noisy as devices pile up, and each screen invents its own language. None is a colour problem — they're hierarchy problems. So: can one visual rule carry both state and importance, and stay calm as the home grows?

03

The big idea

If the trouble is hierarchy, not colour, then the fix has to live in something other than colour.

Every app I looked at leaned on colour and labels — and grew noisier as devices piled up. Demama uses the one cue none of them did: depth.

Depth carries meaning. Raised = available. Pressed = selected.

The idea only matters if it survives contact with real screens. Before the system and the surfaces, here's the product in its setting — calm, low-contrast, nothing competing for attention.

From here the case gets concrete: the architecture, the rejected paths, and every screen the depth rule had to carry.

Four forks
How many things live on the nav bar?
Every room, device and setting wanted a spot on the bar — which would rebuild the exact noise I was escaping.
Rejected

Six or seven items — rooms, devices, scenes, energy, cameras, settings. Thorough, but a scan every time.

Chosen

Four destinations. Rooms and devices fold into Home — the bar is a glance, not a search.

Should a status look like a switch?
On the room grid devices only report their state — but a toggle there promises an action that isn't on this view.
Rejected

A decorative toggle — looks interactive, does nothing here. Every dead tap erodes trust.

Chosen

A labelled status dot — reads as an indicator. The control stays where the action is.

How does a selected item announce itself?
Scenes, climate modes and presets all mark an active choice — usually with a tick or ring, one more mark on a busy screen.
Rejected

An outline ring on the active card — works, but a second visual system over the shadows.

Chosen

The active card is just pressed in — selection rides the same depth rule as everything.

Should heating and cooling use different colours?
The thermostat arc is the Climate centrepiece. Red-heat / blue-cool is the convention — but it flips the screen's anchor by mode.
Rejected

A warm arc for heat, cool for cool — familiar, but the anchor changes colour on you.

Chosen

One blue arc, both modes — mode named in a pressed card below. The centrepiece stays quiet.

07

Outcome & reflection

What the depth rule actually bought, where it stops, and what I'd test before trusting it.

One rule — raised is available, pressed is selected — carried across every screen without a single border or second accent. The busiest screen stays as quiet as the simplest: depth tells controls from status before a label is read.

Honest scope

This is a concept. It never shipped and hasn't been tested with real users, so "calmer" and "faster to read" are still claims, not findings. Whether people decode pressed-as-selected without a legend is the open question.

What I learned
01

A constraint beats a palette

Picking one cue and refusing the rest beat any colour scheme. The hard part wasn't drawing screens — it was saying no to the second signal every time.

02

Soft UI fights accessibility

Neumorphism lives on low contrast; legibility needs the opposite. I kept the look but had to push ink and edges past where the style "wanted" them — a compromise, not a clean win.

03

Decide once, reuse everywhere

Early on I reinvented a component instead of pulling it from the system, and it showed. Building the rules first, then composing from them, made every later screen faster and the whole thing cohere.

If I took it further

Contrast audit. Run every text and state pair against WCAG AA — soft UI is exactly where that breaks.

Test the core claim. Put the prototype in front of people and watch whether depth alone reads as state, with no label.

Motion spec. Define the press transition — how a tile sinks and settles is half of what sells "selected."

Dark theme. Re-derive the two elevations on a dark surface, where neumorphic shadows behave differently.

The end

Thanks for watching.

And thanks for scrolling all the way to the bottom — depth all the way down.
